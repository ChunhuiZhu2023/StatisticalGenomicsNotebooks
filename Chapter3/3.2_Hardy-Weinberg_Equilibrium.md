# 3.2 Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium

## 3.2.1 The law of Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium

In a large random mating population without disruptive effects, such as selection, mutation, and migration, the gene and genotype frequencies remain constant from one generation to the next. This equilibrium state was discovered independently by Hardy and Weinberg in 1908, thus named as Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE). The verification of the Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) law is straightforward. Table 3.1 presents a comprehensive overview of the genotype frequencies in the subsequent generation. Thus, 

$$P_{offspring} = p^4 + 2p^3q + p^2q^2 = p^2(p^2 + 2pq + q^2) = p^2$$

$$H_{offspring} = 2p^3q + 2p^2q^2 + 2p^2q^2 + 2p^2q^2 = 2pq(p^2 + 2pq + q^2) = 2pq$$

$$Q_{offspring} = p^2q^2 + 2p^2q^3 + q^4 = q^2(p^2 + 2pq + q^2) = q^2$$

Within the framework of diploid organisms, the alleles that comply with the Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) are distinguished by the following features:\
(1) The genotype frequencies are $P = p^2$, $H = 2pq$, and $Q = q^2$.\
(2) The maximum value of H is 0.5.\
(3) the HWE can be reached at one generation subsequent to random mating.\
(4) If $H^2 = 4PQ$, the three genotype frequencies in the population remain constant.

## 3.2.2 The applications of Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium

A specific implementation of HWE is the estimation of the frequency of a recessive allele. The estimation of the recessive allele frequency can be derived from the following equation: $\hat{q} = \hat{Q} + \hat{H}/2$, for the given three genotype frequencies, $P$, $H$, and $Q$. Assuming that a specific trait, such as the white flower phenotype, is governed by two recessive alleles ($aa$), it is possible to estimate $Q$ by quantifying the proportion of individuals who display the recessive trait. However, the red flower (dominant trait) individuals consist of both $AA$ and $Aa$. The observed frequency of red-flower individuals is $P + H$. However, due to the lack of knowledge regarding H, the estimation of $q$ by $\hat{q} = \hat{Q} + \hat{H}/2$ is not feasible. However, under the assumption of HWE, $Q$ is equivalent to $q^2$. Therefore, $q$ can be calculated by the formula $\hat{q} = \sqrt{\hat{Q}}$, since $\hat{Q} = N_{aa}/N$. 
Furthermore, the HWE can be utilized to ascertain the frequency of carriers. Carriers are defined as individuals who exhibit a dominant trait but carry one recessive allele, i.e., $Aa$. Within the domain of genetics, the term "frequency of carrier" is employed to denote the conditional frequency of the heterozygote among the dominant individuals. The distinction between $Aa$ and $AA$ is not possible due to the phenomenon of dominance; however, the frequency of the carrier can be estimated with the framework of HWE. Mathematically,

$$ f = \frac{H}{P + H} = \frac{2pq}{p^2 + 2pq} = \frac{2(1-q)q}{(1-q)^2 + 2(1 - q)q} = \frac{2q}{1+q}$$

## 3.2.3 Chi-Square Test of Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium

The primary distinction between the Chi-square test of HWE and the conventional Chi-square test with given probabilities is addressed by the necessity to estimate the HW-predicted genotype frequencies. As previously defined, the allele frequencies are estimated as follows:

$$\hat{p} = \frac{2N_{AA} + N_{Aa}}{2N} = \frac{2 \times 123 + 249}{2 \times 500} = 0.495$$

$$\hat{q} = \frac{2N_{aa} + N_{Aa}}{2N} = \frac{2 \times 128 + 249}{2 \times 500} = 0.505$$

The expected number for AA is $\hat{p}^2 \times 500 = 122.5125$; the expected number for Aa is $\hat{p} \times \hat{q} \times 500 = 249.975$; the expected number for aa is $\hat{q}^2 \times 500 = 127.5125$. Finally, the chi-square test statistic for the goodness of fit with three genotypes is 

$${\chi}^2 = \displaystyle\sum \frac{(O - E)^2}{E} = \frac{(123-122.5125)^2}{122.5125} + \frac{(249 - 249.975)^2}{249.975} + \frac{(128 - 127.5125)^2}{127.5125} \approx 0.0076$$
The chi-squared statistic is less than 3.84, which is the 95% of the Chi-square on distribution, indicating that the data does not exhibit a significant degree of deviation from the expected distribution. Consequently, the population is in HWE. It is important to note that the degree of freedom should be set to 1, rather than 2. This adjustment is necessary because the estimation of allele frequency using the available data has resulted in the loss of an additional degree of freedom.
