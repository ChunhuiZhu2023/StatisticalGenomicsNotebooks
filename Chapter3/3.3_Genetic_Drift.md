# 3.3 Genetic Drift

Genetic drift is a genetic phenomenon in which the frequency of a particular allele in a population undergoes a change due to random sampling of organisms. The term "genetic drift" was proposed by Sewall Wright, a pioneer in the field of population genetics, in 1929. Genetic drift is driven by genetic sampling error within a finite population. To exemplify, in a population with a frequency of p = 0.5 for the allele A and q = 0.5 for the allele a, under the assumption of an infinite population size, the expected frequency of each allele would remain at 0.5, according to the HWE. Conversely, in instances of minimal sample size, e.g., a mere five individuals (N = 5) engaged in random mating to produce an infinite progeny for the next generation. The allele frequency of A is thus determined by the number of A alleles carried by the five sampled parents. Consider a subpopulation of five individuals with genotypes designated as AA, Aa, Aa, aa, and aa. The number of A alleles in this population is four, resulting in p = 0.4, which is not equal to 0.5. The observed shift in p from 0.5 to 0.4 can be attributed to the phenomenon of genetic drift. The probability of observing k alleles of type A is determined by the Wright-Fisher model

$$\frac{(2N)!}{k!(2N - k)!}p^kq^{2N - k}$$

In the aforementioned example, the value of k is 4 and the value of N is 5. Therefore, the probability is 

$$\frac{(2 \times 5)!}{4!(2 \times 5 - 4)!}(\frac{1}{2})^4(\frac{1}{2})^{2 \times 5 - 4} = 0.2050781$$

In order to ensure the maintenance of the frequency of 0.5 for the next generation, it is necessary that there be k = 5 A alleles for the five parents, which has a probability of

$$\frac{(2 \times 5)!}{5!(2 \times 5 - 5)!}(\frac{1}{2})^5(\frac{1}{2})^{2 \times 5 - 5} = 0.4921875$$

Genetic draft has the potential to induce fixation or loss of an allele, even in the absence of selection. As an illustration, the five sampled individuals may all be of type AA, a scenario where A is fixed (a is lost). The probability of A being fixed is 

$$\frac{(2 \times 5)!}{10!(2 \times 5 - 10)!}(\frac{1}{2})^10(\frac{1}{2})^{2 \times 5 - 10} =0.0009765625$$

Despite the fact that the probability appears to be negligible, when considering the effects of multiple generations, the fixation probability will ultimately reach 0.5, which corresponds to the initial gene frequency of A. 

The amount of random drift, at any given generation, can be measured by the variance of allele frequencies over the subpopulations (lines). It is hypothesized that $q_k$ is the allele frequency for a from the $k^{th}$ lines. The variance is defined as

$$σ_q^2 = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{k = 1}^m {(q_k - q)}^2$$

where m refers to the number of subpopulations and $q = 1 - p$ is the initial frequency of allele a. When $q$ is 
unknown, it can be replaced by the mean of the m subpopulations. In this case, the variance is defined as

$$σ_q^2 = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{k = 1}^m {(q_k - \bar{q})}^2$$

with the mean allele frequency $\bar{q}$ is calculated as the mean of the whole population.

The consequence of genetic drift is the reduction in heterozygosity. Here, the variable is denoted by $q$ (allele frequency of a subpopulation, not the initial frequency) and the variance of this variable is expressed by

$$σ_q^2 = E(q^2) - E^2(q)$$

In the absence of population subdivision, it is assumed that $σ_q^2 = 0$, i.e., $E(q^2) = E^2(q)$. This assumption is equivalent to the frequency of homozygosity for genotype aa. Nonetheless, in the context of population subdivision, $E(q^2)$ persists as the frequency of homozygosity, while $E^2(q)$ does not, and

$$E(q^2) = E^2(q) + σ_q^2$$

In the absence of knowledge regarding population subdivision, it is reasonable to hypothesize that the homozygosity of the entire population is equivalent to $E^2(q)$. Given that $E(q^2)$ exceeds $E^2(q)$, it is deduced that there has been a gain of homozygosity or loss of heterozygosity. This phenomenon was first observed by Swedish geneticist Sten Wahlund in 1928 and is known as the Wahlund effect. To simplify the notation, let $\bar{q}$ denote $E(q)$. As illustrated in Table 3.3, a discrepancy exists between the genotype frequencies observed within the subdivided population and the hypothetical pooled population.

Table 3.3 Genotype frequencies of a subdivided population, a hypothetic pooled population and a population with inbreeding

|Genotype|Subdivided|Pooled|Inbreeding|
|:------:|:--------:|:----:|:--------:|
|$AA$|$\bar{p}^2 + σ_q^2$|$\bar{p}^2$|$\bar{p}^2$ + $\bar{pq}F$|
|$Aa$|$2\bar{pq} - 2σ_q^2$|$2\bar{pq}$|$2\bar{pq} -2\bar{pq}F$|
|$aa$|$\bar{q}^2 + σ_q^2$|$\bar{q}^2$|$\bar{q}^2 + \bar{pq}F$|



The genotype frequencies of the pooled population are hypothetical in nature, as they are contingent upon the entire population exhibiting Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE). In the actual subdivided population, the genotypic frequencies deviate from the HWE.

Genetic drift in a subdivided population exhibits a similar pattern to that of inbreeding, as evidenced in the last column of Table 3.3, which presents the genotypic frequencies in a population with an average inbreeding coefficient of F, with $σ_q^2 = \bar{pq}F$. While inbreeding and genetic drift emerge as distinct concepts, they ultimately result in analogous outcomes for the target population. For this reason, $F$ is frequently employed to denote the degree of population divergence, defined as 

$$F = \frac{σ_q^2}{\bar{pq}}$$

which is referred as fixation index or $F_{ST}$. Inbreeding is defined as the mating of relatives. Consequently, the two alleles carried by a progeny may be a copy of the same allele in the history. The presence of two such alleles in a given individual indicates an underlying identity by descent (IBD). The probability of IBD of the two alleles in the progeny is denoted by the inbreeding coefficient, and will be addressed later.
