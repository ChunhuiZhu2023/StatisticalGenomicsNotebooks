# 3.4 Wright’s F statistics

The $F_ST$ previously mentioned falls into the category of $F$ statistics, which were developed by Wright to describe evolutionary behaviors of structured populations. Wright proposed three $F$ statistics: $F_{IT}$, $F_{ST}$, and $F_{IS}$. $F_{IT}$ is referred to as the correlation coefficient of the two alleles within individuals; $F_{ST}$ is the correlation coefficient between two alleles from different individuals within a population; $F_{IS}$, is the correlation of the two alleles within individuals within a population. 

Malecot defined the correlation coefficient between two alleles as the probability that the two alleles are IBD. This concept is referred to as the fixation index. According to this framework, the $F_{IT}$ is analogous to the probability that the two alleles from the same individuals are IBD within the base population, which is defined as the ancestors of all individuals in all populations. $F_{IS}$ is the probability that the two alleles from the same individuals are IBD within the base population, which is defined as the population when isolation first began. $F_{ST}$ is the probability that a random allele from one population is IBD to a random allele from another population. 

When the $F$ statistics are defined as fixation indices, $1 – F$ becomes a heterozygosity reduction. It is hypothesized that $H_0$ will represent the heterozygosity, defined as the proportion of heterozygotes, in the initial state. The heterozygosity at the point of population divergence is expressed as $H_1 = (1 - F_{ST})H_0$. The heterozygosity in the end $H_2 = (1 - F_{IS})H_1 = (1 - F_{IS})(1 - F_{ST})H_0 = (1 - F_{IT})H_0$, in agree with the famous equation in population genetics and evolution

$$(1 - F_{IT}) = (1 - F_{IS})(1 - F_{ST})$$

The equation $F_{ST} = σ_q^2 / \bar{pq}$ is the definition formula for population differentiation (fixation index). The formula itself can be used to estimate $F_{ST}; however, a more accurate estimate of $F_{ST} is Cockerham's hierarchical analysis of variance (ANOVA). This analysis utilizes a nested design of analysis of variance, where $i = 1,…,m$ indexes the population, $j = 1,…,n$ indexes an individual within the population, and $k = 1,2$ indexes an allele within the same individual. Subsequently, 

$$
y_{ijk} =
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{for A}\\
0 & \text{for a}
\end{cases}
$$

serves as the allelic indicator for the $k^{th}$ allele of the $j^{th}$ individual within population $i$. Cockerham employs the following linear model to describe this allelic indicator variable:

$$ y_{ijk} = μ + α_i + β_{(i)j} + γ_{(ij)k}$$

where $μ$ is the population mean (frequency of $A$ in the entire population), $α_i$ is the mean of $y$ in the $i^{th}$ population, expressed as a deviation from the mean of the whole population, $β_{(i)j}$ is the mean of $y$ in the $j^{th}$ individual within the ith population, expressed as a deviation from the mean of this population, and $y_{ijk}$ is the residual (the allelic indicator expressed as a deviation from all previous terms). It is hypothesized that all terms in the model, with the exception of $μ$, are random variables. Under this assumption, the variances of the parameters $α_i$, $β_{(i)j}$, and $γ_{(i,j)k}$ are equivalent to $σ_α^2$, $σ_β^2$, and $σ_γ^2$, respectively. Therefore, the total variance of $y$ is expressed as follows:

$$var(y_{ijk}) = σ_y^2 = σ_α^2 + σ_β^2 + σ_γ^2$$

The following is a proposed definition of $F$ statistics in terms of correlation coefficients. $F_{IT}$ is used to denote the correlation coefficient between the two alleles of an individual. From a statistical perspective, the correlation is defined as 

$$ r_{y_{ijk},y_{ijk'}}=\frac{cov(y_{ijk},y_{ijk'})}{\sqrt{var(y_{ijk})var(y_{ijk'})}} $$

where $k'$ is not equal to $k$, and both $y$ have the same subscript $j$ (meaning the same individual). The numerator is thus represented by 

$$cov(y_{ijk},y_{ijk'}) = cov(μ + α_i + β_{(i)j} + γ_{(ij)k}, μ + α_i + β_{(i)j} + γ_{(ij)k'})=cov(α_i, α_i) + cov(β_{(i)j}, β_{(i)j})= var(α_i) + var(β_{(i)j}) = σ_α^2 + σ_β^2$$

Therefore,

$$ F_{IT} = r_{y_{ijk},y_{ijk'}} = \frac{σ_α^2 + σ_β^2}{σ_α^2 + σ_β^2 + σ_γ^2}$$

The $F_{ST}$ parameter is defined as the correlation coefficient between two alleles from two different individuals within the same population, and is denoted by

$$ r_{y_{ijk},y_{ij'k'}} = \frac{cov(y_{ijk},y_{ij'k'})}{\sqrt{var(y_{ijk})var(y_{ij'k'})}} $$

The covariance in the numerator is  

$$cov(y_{ijk},y_{ij'k'}) = cov(μ + α_i + β_{(i)j} + γ_{(ij)k}, μ + α_i + β_{(i)j'} + γ_{(ij)k'})=cov(α_i, α_i)= σ_α^2$$

Therefore,

$$ F_{ST} = r_{y_{ijk},y_{ij'k'}} = \frac{σ_α^2}{σ_α^2 + σ_β^2 + σ_γ^2}$$

The $F_{IS}$ parameter is defined as the correlation coefficient between two alleles from the same individual within the same population. This indicates that the focus is on a single population, with the parameter being the average of the parameter across all populations. In this investigation, the model is revisited by focusing on a single population, thus eliminating subscript $i$, i.e., 

$$ y_{jk} = μ + β_{j} + γ_{(j)k}$$
$$var(y_{jk}) = σ_y^2 = σ_β^2 + σ_γ^2$$

The correlation coefficient between the two alleles from the same individual is denoted as 

$$ r_{y_{jk},y_{jk'}}=\frac{cov(y_{jk},y_{jk'})}{\sqrt{var(y_{jk})var(y_{jk'})}} $$

The covariance in the numerator is 

$$cov(y_{jk},y_{jk'}) = cov(μ + β_{j} + γ_{(j)k}, μ + + β_{j} + γ_{(j)k'}) = cov(β_{j}, β_{j}) = σ_β^2$$

Therefore,

$$ F_{IS} = r_{y_{jk},y_{j'k'}} = \frac{σ_β^2}{σ_β^2 + σ_γ^2}$$

In accordance with the linear model outlined above, it is possible to sample allelic data from individuals within populations. Each data point represents an allelic state, defined as a binary variable that can take a value of 0 or 1. In SNP data, there are only two alleles (multiple alleles are very rare) and the "reference allele" is code 1. The designation of the specific allele as the reference allele is determined by the investigator's discretion, and is not predetermined or fixed. The ANOVA table can be found in Table 3.4. The population sizes are not uniform, and thus the data is considered as unbalanced. Indeed, in the context of population differentiation analysis, the population is invariably imbalanced. The "average"" number of individuals of a population is calculated differently from the usual definition of average. The calculation is performed using the 

$$ k_0 = \frac{1}{m-1}(\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^m n_i - \frac{\sum_{i=1}^m n_i^2}{\sum_{i=1}^m n_i})$$

Subsequently, the three variance components are estimated using

$$ σ_γ^2 = MS_γ$$

$$ σ_β^2 = \frac{1}{2}(MS_β - MS_γ)$$

$$ σ_α^2 = \frac{k_0}{MS_α - MS_β}$$
