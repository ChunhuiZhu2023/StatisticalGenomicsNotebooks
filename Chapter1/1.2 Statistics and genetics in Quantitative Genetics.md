# 1.2 Statistics and genetics in Quantitative Genetics

  <p style="text-align: justify; text-indent: 2em;">
    Quantitative traits are continuously varying, polygenic, and environmentally influenced traits. Despite the fact that, in theory, the segregation pattern of a single locus and its effect on a quantitative trait are still guided by Mendel's law of inheritance if all other loci fixed, the collective effect of all genes, in conjunction with the impact of environmental factors, ultimately overshadows the discrete nature of the Mendelian loci. Consequently, the analysis of the inheritance of quantitative traits demands the utilization of more advanced statistical tools. It could be argued that a comprehensive understanding of Mendel's law of inheritance and the concept of linkage disequilibrium between the loci is often sufficient for a grasp of the principles of quantitative genetics. However, the pursuit of studies in quantitative genetics demands a substantially more profound statistical foundation, encompassing, but not limited to, distributions, expectations, variances, correlations, (generalized) linear models, (generalized) linear mixed models, maximum likelihood estimation, statistical tests, Bayesian statistics, and many others.
  </p>

  <p style="text-align: justify; text-indent: 2em;">
    Throughout history, the evolution of statistics and genetics has been inextricably linked. Quantitative genetics is predicated on the principles of population genetics. The Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium constitutes the underlying principle upon which genetic variance is categorized into two distinct components: additive variance and dominance variance. Linkage disequilibrium (LD) is the fundamental principle underpinning quantitative trait locus (QTL) mapping. The fundamental concept underpinning the calculation of the kinship matrix of genetically related individuals is the Identity-by-Descent (IBD) model, which describes the relationship between alleles. This methodology facilitates the estimation of the genetic variance for a quantitative trait. The three pioneers of population genetics (R.A. Fisher, J.B.S. Haldane and S. Wright) were also renowned mathematicians and statisticians. Furthermore, a series of statistical theories were formulated in order to address issues in genetics. Fisher developed the technology of analysis of variances (ANOVA) to address the issue of human stature, thereby describing the standard deviation of human stature by $σ = \sqrt{σ_B^2 + σ_B^2}$, with the partitioning of variance into between-family ($σ_B^2$) and within-family ($σ_W^2$) components. The theory of regression was initially developed by Galton in his research on the inheritance of human stature (the laws of ancestral heredity). The theoretical framework of linear mixed models was pioneered by Henderson in the 1950s for the estimation of genetic parameters and the prediction of breeding values in unbalanced populations of large animals.
  </p>
